## 2. Accuracy fixes before we record

The memo's tone is right, but a few lines would be wrong on screen, and accountants in the comments will catch them:

| In the memo | Fix |
|---|---|
| "The left side are your liabilities and equity" | Liabilities and equity sit on the right side (credit balances). This is a slip of the tongue. |
| "Balance sheet: left side is good, right side is bad" | Equity going up is good, and it's a credit. Use a rule that's always true instead: assets, expenses and dividends increase with debits; liabilities, equity and revenue increase with credits (the "DEALER" rule). |
| "Debits are what you get, credits are what gets taken away" | Keep it as a starter hook, but tighten it to "debit = where the money went, credit = where it came from." |
| "50 cents on the debit side for inventory expense" | When you buy the water: Dr Inventory 0.50 / Cr Accounts Payable 0.50. When you sell it: Dr Cost of Goods Sold 0.50 / Cr Inventory 0.50. |

The script below keeps your voice and fixes these.

## 3. Mock script: about 4:00 (about 540 words at your 147 wpm, plus two 5-second quiz pauses)

**[0:00 Cold open]**
Debits and credits suck. I've been in accounting for over twenty-five years, and the first time I saw them I thought they were pointless. Give me four minutes and I'll show you the picture that finally made them click.

**[0:15 Intro card: your photo]**
I'm the AI Business 101 Professor. On this channel we cover AI, accounting basics, accounting standards, and a little financial planning and analysis. Let's go.

**[0:25 The story]**
Early in my career, my manager would walk up to the whiteboard, draw a big T, and start working an accounting problem. And I'd think: what the heck? I learned this in my very first accounting class. But the longer I've been in this career, the more I use that T. It's the fastest way to think through any transaction.

**[0:50 The T-account]**
This is a T-account. It's literally a T. The left side is debits. The right side is credits. That's it. Debit means left, credit means right. Nothing about good or bad.

**[1:05 The rule]**
Here's the rule that never breaks. Assets, expenses and dividends go up with a debit. Liabilities, equity and revenue go up with a credit. A starter hook: a debit shows where the money went, a credit shows where it came from.

**[1:30 Quiz 1: countdown]**
Quick quiz. Your cash goes up. Debit or credit? … Debit. Cash is an asset, and assets go up on the left.

**[1:45 Example 1: sell the water]**
Let's sell something. A bottle of water, one dollar, and the customer pays cash. Two T-accounts: Cash and Revenue. Cash went up, so one dollar goes on the debit side of Cash. We earned revenue, so one dollar goes on the credit side of Revenue. Side by side: one dollar left, one dollar right. Debits equal credits, every single time.

**[2:25 Example 2: buy the water]**
But where did that water come from? We bought it for fifty cents, and we haven't paid the supplier yet. Inventory goes up: debit fifty cents. We owe the supplier: credit Accounts Payable fifty cents. When we sell the bottle, that fifty cents moves out of inventory and into Cost of Goods Sold. Revenue one dollar, cost fifty cents. We just made fifty cents of profit.

**[3:15 Quiz 2: countdown]**
Your turn. We pay the supplier the fifty cents in cash. Which account gets the debit? … Accounts Payable. We owe less, so the liability goes down with a debit, and cash goes down with a credit.

**[3:35 Recap]**
So: debit is left, credit is right. Every entry has both, and they always match. Draw the T and you'll never get lost.

**[3:50 Close]**
Next lesson: why a profitable business can still run out of cash. Drop your quiz answers in the comments and I'll tell you if you got them right.
